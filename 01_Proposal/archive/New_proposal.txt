INTRODUCTION

1.1 Background

Human Activity Recognition (HAR) has become one of the most active
research areas in artificial intelligence, wearable computing, and
healthcare due to its ability to automatically identify human physical
activities from sensor data. Recent advances in wearable sensing
technologies, including smartphones, smartwatches, fitness trackers, and
inertial measurement units (IMUs), have enabled continuous monitoring of
human behavior in real-world environments. Such technologies play an
essential role in numerous applications, including healthcare
monitoring, elderly assistance, rehabilitation, smart homes, sports
analytics, occupational safety, and human-computer interaction.

Unlike vision-based activity recognition systems, wearable sensor-based
HAR offers several advantages, including lower computational cost,
better privacy preservation, robustness under different lighting
conditions, and continuous monitoring without requiring cameras.
Consequently, wearable sensing has become the preferred solution for
many real-time intelligent healthcare applications.

Recent advances in deep learning have significantly improved HAR
performance. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have demonstrated
excellent capability in extracting local spatial features from raw
inertial sensor signals, while Transformer architectures have shown
remarkable success in modeling long-range temporal dependencies. These
models have substantially increased recognition accuracy across
benchmark datasets, with many recent studies reporting accuracies
exceeding 95%.

Despite these achievements, current HAR systems still rely heavily on a
conventional processing pipeline that segments continuous sensor streams
into fixed-length sliding windows before feature extraction and activity
classification. Although simple and computationally efficient, this
segmentation strategy introduces artificial temporal boundaries that
rarely correspond to the actual beginning and ending of human
activities. Consequently, activities with variable durations,
overlapping movements, and transitional motions are frequently
fragmented into multiple windows or merged with neighboring activities,
leading to ambiguous feature representations and reduced recognition
performance.

Furthermore, modern deep learning models require large quantities of
accurately labeled training data. Constructing such datasets is
extremely expensive because sensor annotations must be performed
manually by domain experts, particularly for transitional activities
whose temporal boundaries are often subjective and difficult to define
consistently. As a result, the scalability of supervised HAR systems
remains limited when deployed in real-world scenarios.

Another emerging challenge concerns the recognition of transitional
activities, such as sit-to-stand, stand-to-sit, walking-to-running, or
turning movements. These activities typically last only a few seconds,
exhibit substantial inter-subject variability, and occur precisely at
the boundaries between consecutive activities. Existing HAR systems
generally focus on recognizing steady-state activities while treating
transition periods as noise or misclassification regions, despite their
critical importance in healthcare monitoring, fall prevention,
rehabilitation assessment, and elderly care.

These limitations indicate that future HAR systems should move beyond
conventional activity classification toward understanding the semantic
structure of continuous human behavior. Instead of relying on manually
designed segmentation strategies and extensive labeled datasets,
intelligent systems should automatically discover meaningful activity
boundaries, learn robust activity representations from unlabeled sensor
streams, and accurately recognize both steady and transitional
activities within a unified learning framework.

Recent developments in self-supervised learning have demonstrated
remarkable potential for learning meaningful representations without
requiring extensive manual annotations. By exploiting intrinsic
relationships within unlabeled sensor signals, self-supervised learning
enables models to acquire generalized activity representations that can
later be adapted to downstream recognition tasks using only limited
labeled data. Similarly, attention-based temporal modeling provides an
effective mechanism for capturing contextual relationships across long
sensor sequences, making it particularly suitable for identifying
activity boundaries and modeling transitional movements.

Motivated by these observations, this research proposes a Boundary-Aware
Self-Supervised Human Activity Recognition (BASS-HAR) Framework, a
unified architecture that integrates semantic boundary discovery,
boundary-guided self-supervised representation learning, and
context-aware temporal modeling to address the fundamental limitations
of current HAR systems. Unlike existing approaches that treat
segmentation, representation learning, and activity recognition as
independent processes, the proposed framework jointly optimizes these
components within a single end-to-end architecture. The framework aims
to automatically discover semantic activity boundaries, learn
discriminative representations from largely unlabeled wearable sensor
data, and improve the recognition of challenging transitional activities
while maintaining high recognition performance for continuous human
activity recognition.

This research is expected to contribute not only to the advancement of
wearable sensor-based HAR but also to broader applications of
intelligent healthcare, ambient assisted living, and next-generation
context-aware wearable computing systems.

1.2 Research Motivation

The rapid growth of wearable sensing technologies has created
unprecedented opportunities for continuous human activity monitoring
across healthcare, smart living, rehabilitation, and personalized
fitness applications. Modern wearable devices are now capable of
continuously collecting large volumes of inertial sensor data from
individuals during their daily activities. This continuous sensing
capability has shifted the focus of Human Activity Recognition (HAR)
research from laboratory-based experiments toward real-world intelligent
monitoring systems.

Despite remarkable progress in deep learning-based HAR, existing systems
still suffer from several fundamental limitations that restrict their
practical deployment. Most current approaches continue to rely on
manually defined fixed-size sliding windows to segment continuous sensor
streams before classification. Although computationally simple,
fixed-window segmentation ignores the natural temporal boundaries of
human activities and often splits a single activity into multiple
windows or merges different activities into one segment. Consequently,
important temporal information, particularly during activity
transitions, is lost before the learning process even begins.

Another significant challenge is the strong dependence of modern HAR
models on large-scale labeled datasets. High-quality annotation of
wearable sensor data is labor-intensive, time-consuming, and often
requires expert knowledge to determine the precise start and end of each
activity. This challenge becomes even more severe for transitional
activities, whose boundaries are inherently ambiguous and vary
considerably among different individuals. As wearable sensing
applications continue to expand into healthcare and ambient assisted
living, collecting sufficiently labeled datasets for every environment,
user population, and activity type becomes increasingly impractical.

Recent advances in self-supervised learning have demonstrated that deep
neural networks can learn meaningful representations directly from
unlabeled data by exploiting intrinsic temporal structures within the
data itself. However, most existing self-supervised HAR approaches still
inherit the limitations of fixed-window segmentation, causing the
learned representations to depend on arbitrary temporal partitions
rather than meaningful activity structures. Consequently, the potential
of self-supervised learning has not yet been fully exploited for
continuous human activity understanding.

In parallel, transitional activities have received relatively limited
attention despite their critical importance in healthcare applications.
Movements such as sit-to-stand, stand-to-sit, turning, and gait
transitions are essential indicators of mobility, physical
rehabilitation progress, fall risk, and neurological disorders.
Unfortunately, these activities remain among the most difficult to
recognize because they are short in duration, highly variable among
subjects, and frequently occur near activity boundaries where
conventional segmentation strategies perform poorly.

These observations reveal that the current research direction in HAR
should move beyond merely improving classification accuracy. Instead,
future intelligent HAR systems should be capable of understanding the
semantic structure of continuous human behavior by automatically
discovering activity boundaries, learning robust representations from
largely unlabeled sensor streams, and recognizing transitional movements
within a unified framework.

Motivated by these challenges, this research proposes a Boundary-Aware
Self-Supervised Human Activity Recognition (BASS-HAR) framework that
integrates semantic boundary discovery, boundary-guided self-supervised
representation learning, and context-aware temporal modeling into a
unified end-to-end architecture. By jointly optimizing these components
rather than treating them as independent stages, the proposed framework
aims to improve recognition accuracy, reduce dependence on labeled data,
and significantly enhance the detection of transitional activities in
continuous wearable sensor streams.

Ultimately, this research is motivated by the vision of developing
next-generation HAR systems that not only recognize human activities but
also understand the temporal semantics of continuous human behavior,
enabling more reliable and intelligent wearable sensing applications for
healthcare, rehabilitation, and smart environments.

1.3 Problem Statement

Human Activity Recognition (HAR) has achieved remarkable progress over
the past decade due to advances in deep learning and wearable sensing
technologies. Deep neural networks, particularly Convolutional Neural
Networks (CNNs) and Transformer-based architectures, have significantly
improved activity recognition accuracy on benchmark datasets. However,
despite these advances, existing HAR systems remain largely dependent on
traditional processing pipelines that limit their effectiveness in
continuous real-world environments.

The first major limitation lies in the use of fixed-size sliding window
segmentation. Most existing HAR models divide continuous sensor streams
into predefined temporal windows before feature extraction and
classification. Although this strategy simplifies data processing, it
assumes that human activities occur within fixed temporal intervals, an
assumption that rarely holds in real-life scenarios. Human activities
naturally vary in duration, and their transitions occur at irregular
time points. Consequently, fixed-window segmentation often fragments a
single activity into multiple segments or merges different activities
into the same window, resulting in ambiguous feature representations and
inaccurate recognition.

The second limitation concerns the heavy dependence of current deep
learning models on large-scale labeled datasets. State-of-the-art HAR
systems generally require thousands of manually annotated sensor samples
to achieve competitive performance. Producing such annotations is both
labor-intensive and time-consuming, especially for continuous sensor
streams where accurately identifying activity boundaries demands expert
knowledge. Furthermore, manually labeling large-scale wearable sensor
data is impractical for many real-world healthcare and smart living
applications, significantly restricting the scalability and
generalization of supervised HAR models.

A further challenge arises from the recognition of transitional
activities, such as sit-to-stand, stand-to-sit, turning, and gait
transitions. These activities are typically short in duration, highly
variable among individuals, and occur precisely at the boundaries
between consecutive activities. Because conventional HAR systems are
primarily optimized for recognizing steady-state activities,
transitional movements are frequently misclassified or ignored. This
limitation is particularly critical in healthcare, elderly monitoring,
rehabilitation, and fall prevention applications, where accurate
recognition of transitional movements is often more important than
recognizing static activities themselves.

Although recent studies have explored dynamic segmentation,
self-supervised learning, and temporal modeling independently, these
research directions remain largely disconnected. Existing dynamic
segmentation approaches generally rely on handcrafted rules or
additional supervision, while current self-supervised HAR models
continue to depend on fixed-window segmentation that ignores semantic
activity boundaries. Likewise, most transitional activity recognition
methods are developed as standalone classification tasks without
exploiting boundary information or learned temporal representations.
Consequently, the relationships among semantic boundary discovery,
representation learning, and transitional activity recognition remain
insufficiently investigated.

Therefore, a significant research gap exists in developing a unified
Human Activity Recognition framework capable of simultaneously
discovering semantic activity boundaries, learning robust activity
representations from unlabeled wearable sensor streams, and accurately
recognizing both steady and transitional activities within continuous
sensor data. Addressing these challenges requires a new learning
paradigm that integrates semantic boundary discovery, self-supervised
representation learning, and context-aware temporal modeling into a
single end-to-end framework rather than treating them as independent
processing stages.

To address this gap, this research proposes a Boundary-Aware
Self-Supervised Human Activity Recognition (BASS-HAR) framework. The
proposed framework aims to automatically identify semantic activity
boundaries directly from continuous sensor streams, exploit
boundary-guided self-supervised learning to reduce dependence on labeled
data, and improve the recognition of transitional activities through
context-aware temporal modeling. By jointly optimizing these components,
the proposed framework seeks to overcome the major limitations of
existing HAR systems and provide a more robust, scalable, and
intelligent solution for continuous human activity understanding.

1.4 Research Gap

Despite the remarkable progress achieved in wearable sensor-based Human
Activity Recognition (HAR), several fundamental research gaps remain
unresolved. Existing studies have primarily focused on improving
classification accuracy through more sophisticated deep learning
architectures, such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), Recurrent
Neural Networks (RNNs), Transformers, and hybrid CNN-Transformer models.
Although these approaches have achieved high recognition performance on
benchmark datasets, they continue to rely on conventional processing
pipelines that restrict their applicability in real-world continuous
activity recognition.

The first research gap concerns activity segmentation. Most existing HAR
systems still depend on fixed-size sliding windows to partition
continuous sensor streams before feature extraction. This strategy
assumes that all activities have similar temporal durations and ignores
the semantic boundaries between consecutive activities. As a result,
activity transitions are frequently divided across multiple windows or
merged into neighboring activities, producing inconsistent feature
representations and reducing recognition performance. Although several
studies have investigated adaptive or dynamic segmentation techniques,
these methods often require handcrafted rules, threshold-based
algorithms, or additional supervision, limiting their scalability and
robustness in real-world environments.

The second research gap relates to representation learning from
unlabeled sensor data. Recent advances in self-supervised learning have
demonstrated promising results by enabling HAR models to learn
discriminative feature representations without requiring extensive
manual annotations. However, most existing self-supervised HAR
approaches continue to generate training samples using conventional
fixed-window segmentation. Consequently, the learned representations
remain constrained by arbitrary temporal partitions rather than
meaningful activity structures. Very limited research has explored how
semantic activity boundaries can guide self-supervised representation
learning to improve feature quality and reduce labeling requirements
simultaneously.

The third research gap concerns the recognition of transitional
activities. Existing HAR research predominantly emphasizes the
recognition of steady-state activities such as walking, sitting,
standing, and running, while transitional movements receive relatively
limited attention. Activities such as sit-to-stand, stand-to-sit,
turning, and gait transitions are inherently short, irregular, and
highly variable among individuals. Since these movements occur around
activity boundaries, they are particularly sensitive to segmentation
errors. Current approaches generally treat transitional activity
recognition as an independent classification problem rather than
integrating boundary information and temporal context into the learning
process.

Furthermore, although dynamic segmentation, self-supervised learning,
and transitional activity recognition have each attracted increasing
research interest, these three research directions have largely evolved
independently. Existing studies typically address only one of these
challenges while overlooking their strong interdependence. To the best
of current knowledge, there remains a lack of a unified HAR framework
that simultaneously performs semantic boundary discovery,
boundary-guided self-supervised representation learning, and accurate
recognition of transitional activities within a single end-to-end
learning architecture.

Therefore, the major research gap addressed in this study is the absence
of an integrated framework capable of jointly learning where an activity
begins and ends, how to learn meaningful activity representations from
largely unlabeled sensor streams, and how to accurately recognize both
steady and transitional activities using the learned semantic
boundaries. Addressing this gap is expected to improve recognition
robustness, reduce dependence on manual labeling, and significantly
enhance the practical deployment of wearable HAR systems in healthcare,
rehabilitation, and intelligent ambient environments.

1.5 Research Questions

Based on the identified research gaps, this study seeks to answer the
following research questions:

RQ1

How can semantic activity boundaries be automatically identified from
continuous wearable sensor streams without relying on fixed-size sliding
window segmentation?

Traditional HAR systems employ predefined sliding windows that fail to
align with the natural temporal structure of human activities. This
research investigates whether semantic boundary discovery can provide a
more accurate representation of continuous activities.

RQ2

How can self-supervised learning be integrated with semantic boundary
information to learn robust activity representations from unlabeled
wearable sensor data while reducing dependence on manual annotations?

Existing self-supervised HAR approaches generally learn from arbitrarily
segmented data. This research explores whether boundary-aware
self-supervised learning can improve representation quality and reduce
labeling requirements.

RQ3

How can contextual temporal information and semantic boundary awareness
improve the recognition of transitional human activities compared with
conventional HAR approaches?

Transitional activities remain one of the most challenging problems in
HAR due to their short duration and high variability. This research
investigates whether incorporating semantic boundaries and temporal
context can significantly improve their recognition.

RQ4

Can a unified Boundary-Aware Self-Supervised Human Activity Recognition
(BASS-HAR) framework simultaneously improve activity segmentation,
representation learning, and transitional activity recognition while
maintaining competitive overall recognition performance?

This question evaluates the effectiveness of integrating semantic
boundary discovery, self-supervised representation learning, and
transitional activity recognition within a single end-to-end framework.

CHAPTER 3

Proposed Methodology

3.1 Research Methodology Overview

This research proposes a novel Boundary-Aware Self-Supervised Human
Activity Recognition (BASS-HAR) framework for continuous wearable
sensor-based Human Activity Recognition. The proposed methodology
integrates semantic boundary detection, self-supervised representation
learning, and transitional activity recognition into a unified
end-to-end architecture.

Unlike conventional HAR systems that first divide sensor data into
fixed-size sliding windows before classification, the proposed framework
begins by automatically identifying semantic activity boundaries
directly from continuous sensor streams. These detected boundaries are
then used to generate adaptive activity segments that preserve the
natural temporal structure of human movements.

The segmented activities are subsequently processed using a hybrid
CNN–Transformer architecture. CNN layers extract discriminative local
spatial features from wearable sensor signals, while Transformer layers
model long-range temporal dependencies across activity sequences.

To reduce dependence on manually labeled datasets, a boundary-aware
self-supervised learning strategy is incorporated into the framework.
The proposed learning mechanism exploits semantic boundary information
to construct meaningful positive and negative learning pairs, enabling
robust feature learning from unlabeled sensor streams.

Finally, a context-aware transitional activity recognition module
utilizes temporal context before and after detected activity boundaries
to accurately classify both steady-state and transitional activities.

The overall methodology consists of six major stages, as illustrated in
Figure X.X.

Stage 1: Data Acquisition and Preprocessing

Continuous sensor signals are collected from wearable inertial sensors,
including tri-axial accelerometers and gyroscopes. Each sensor stream
undergoes preprocessing to remove noise and normalize signal amplitudes.

The preprocessing stage consists of:

- Noise filtering

- Missing value handling

- Signal normalization

- Timestamp synchronization

- Continuous sensor stream construction

The output of this stage is a continuous multivariate time-series
suitable for semantic boundary analysis.

Stage 2: Semantic Boundary Detection

Instead of employing conventional fixed-size sliding windows, the
proposed framework first identifies semantic boundaries that represent
the natural transitions between consecutive activities.

A lightweight Boundary Detection Network analyzes temporal changes
within continuous sensor signals and estimates the probability of
activity transitions at each time step.

The detected boundaries divide the sensor stream into adaptive activity
segments with variable lengths corresponding to actual human movements.

Objective

- Eliminate arbitrary segmentation.

- Preserve activity continuity.

- Improve transition localization.

Stage 3: CNN-Based Local Feature Extraction

Each adaptive activity segment is processed by a lightweight
Convolutional Neural Network.

The CNN extracts local motion patterns such as:

- Body acceleration

- Angular velocity

- Motion frequency

- Local temporal dynamics

Depthwise Separable Convolution is adopted to reduce computational
complexity while maintaining feature extraction capability.

The output is a compact feature representation for each activity
segment.

Stage 4: Transformer-Based Temporal Modeling

The extracted feature sequence is then passed to a Transformer encoder.

The Transformer captures:

- Long-range temporal dependencies

- Contextual relationships

- Sequential motion evolution

- Activity continuity

Unlike RNN-based approaches, the Transformer models the complete
activity sequence simultaneously using self-attention mechanisms.

The resulting temporal representations contain both local and global
activity information.

Stage 5: Boundary-Aware Self-Supervised Representation Learning

This stage represents the primary novelty of the proposed research.

Instead of learning representations from randomly segmented windows, the
framework incorporates semantic boundary information into the
self-supervised learning process.

Three complementary pretext tasks are employed:

5.1 Temporal Contrastive Learning

Positive pairs are generated from neighboring segments belonging to the
same semantic activity.

Negative pairs are selected from different activities separated by
detected boundaries.

This encourages the model to maximize intra-activity similarity while
increasing inter-activity discrimination.

5.2 Continuity Prediction

The model predicts whether two consecutive segments belong to:

- the same activity

- different activities

This enables the encoder to learn transition-aware representations.

5.3 Masked Temporal Modeling

Random portions of the sensor sequence are masked.

The network reconstructs the missing sensor values using surrounding
temporal context.

This task enables the model to understand temporal dynamics without
requiring manual labels.

Stage 6: Context-Aware Transitional Activity Recognition

The learned representations are finally used to classify activities.

Unlike conventional HAR systems, the proposed classifier explicitly
models transitional activities.

The classifier receives:

- Semantic boundary information

- CNN features

- Transformer temporal embeddings

- Self-supervised representations

The final prediction includes both:

- Steady-state activities

- Transitional activities

such as:

- Sit-to-Stand

- Stand-to-Sit

- Walk-to-Run

- Turning

8. Expected Outcomes

The proposed Boundary-Aware Self-Supervised Human Activity Recognition
(BASS-HAR) framework is expected to advance the state-of-the-art in
wearable sensor-based Human Activity Recognition by addressing three
fundamental challenges: semantic activity segmentation, label-efficient
learning, and transitional activity recognition.

The expected outcomes of this research are summarized as follows:

1. Improved Activity Segmentation

The proposed semantic boundary detection module is expected to
automatically identify natural activity transitions, eliminating the
limitations of fixed-size sliding windows. This is anticipated to
produce more meaningful activity segments and improve the temporal
consistency of activity recognition.

2. Reduced Dependence on Labeled Data

The boundary-aware self-supervised learning strategy is expected to
learn robust activity representations from unlabeled sensor streams,
significantly reducing the need for expensive manual annotations while
maintaining competitive recognition performance.

3. Enhanced Transitional Activity Recognition

By explicitly modeling activity boundaries and temporal context, the
proposed framework is expected to substantially improve the recognition
of short-duration transitional activities, such as sit-to-stand and
stand-to-sit, which remain challenging for existing HAR systems.

4. Improved Generalization

The combination of semantic segmentation and self-supervised
representation learning is expected to improve the model's robustness
across different users, sensor placements, and benchmark datasets.

5. State-of-the-Art Performance

The proposed framework is expected to outperform existing CNN-based,
Transformer-based, and self-supervised HAR models across multiple public
benchmark datasets using standard evaluation metrics.

6. Practical Research Framework

The proposed BASS-HAR architecture is expected to provide a flexible and
extensible framework that can be adapted for healthcare monitoring,
elderly assistance, smart homes, rehabilitation, and other wearable
computing applications.

9. Novelty of the Proposed Research

The originality of this research lies in the integration of three
complementary research directions into a unified Human Activity
Recognition framework. While each individual component has been
investigated separately in previous studies, their combined integration
remains largely unexplored.

The proposed research introduces the following novel contributions:

Novelty 1: Semantic Boundary-Aware Activity Segmentation

Unlike traditional HAR systems that rely on fixed-size sliding windows,
the proposed framework automatically detects semantic activity
boundaries and generates adaptive activity segments that better
represent natural human movements.

Novelty 2: Boundary-Aware Self-Supervised Learning

This research proposes a novel self-supervised learning strategy that
incorporates semantic boundary information into the representation
learning process. Instead of generating training pairs randomly,
positive and negative pairs are constructed based on detected activity
boundaries, enabling the model to learn more discriminative temporal
representations from unlabeled sensor data.

Novelty 3: Context-Aware Transitional Activity Recognition

The proposed framework explicitly models transitional activities by
utilizing temporal information before and after detected activity
boundaries. This approach enables more accurate recognition of brief and
highly variable transition movements that are often overlooked by
existing HAR systems.

Novelty 4: Unified End-to-End Framework

Rather than addressing segmentation, representation learning, and
activity recognition as separate problems, this research integrates all
components into a unified end-to-end architecture that jointly optimizes
activity segmentation, feature learning, and classification.

Novelty 5: Comprehensive Experimental Validation

The proposed framework will be extensively evaluated through
cross-dataset experiments, low-label learning scenarios,
transition-specific evaluations, and ablation studies to demonstrate the
effectiveness of each proposed contribution.
